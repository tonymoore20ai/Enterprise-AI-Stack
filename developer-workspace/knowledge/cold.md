# Cold weather safety

Source: https://www.weather.gov/dlh/extremecold
Source: https://www.weather.gov/bou/windchill

## Wind chill
Wind chill describes how quickly wind and cold together pull heat from exposed skin, so it can feel far colder than the air temperature. At a wind chill of -20°F, frostbite can set in on exposed skin in about 30 minutes.

## How to dress
- Wear several loose, lightweight layers; the air trapped between layers insulates.
- For active outdoor work, avoid cotton, which stays wet and draws heat away. Choose fabrics that wick moisture and dry quickly.
- The outer layer should be tightly woven, water-repellent and hooded.
- Wear a hat; a large share of body heat is lost through the head. Cover your mouth to protect your lungs from very cold air.
- Mittens keep hands warmer than gloves; they should fit snugly at the wrist.
- Stay dry and out of the wind as much as possible.

## Frostbite
Signs: numbness and white or pale skin, most often on fingers, toes, ear lobes and the tip of the nose.
What to do: get medical help. Until help arrives, rewarm the affected areas gently. If the person also shows signs of hypothermia, warm the body core first, before hands and feet.

## Hypothermia
Hypothermia is a body temperature below 95°F (35°C) and can be fatal.
Signs: uncontrollable shivering, memory loss, confusion, slurred speech, drowsiness and apparent exhaustion.
What to do: if the body temperature is below 95°F, get medical care immediately. Meanwhile, move the person somewhere warm, replace wet clothing with dry clothing and wrap them in warm blankets, including the head and neck. Warm the core of the body first; warming arms and legs first can push cold blood toward the heart. Do not give alcohol, drugs, coffee or hot food and drink; warm broth is a good first food.

## Travel in cold weather
Keep a winter kit in the car: warm clothes, boots, blankets, a flashlight with spare batteries and high-energy snacks. Carry a charged phone and tell someone your route and expected arrival time.
